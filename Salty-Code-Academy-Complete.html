01 · The foundation

The one mental model behind everything

A website is not a brochure. It is a salesperson that works every hour of every day.

Everything else in this pack hangs on that single idea. When you judge a website, do not ask "is it pretty?" Ask "would I hire this person to sell for me?" A salesperson who takes forever to answer the door, mumbles about themselves instead of listening, and never asks for the sale would be fired in a week. Most websites are exactly that salesperson.

A good salesperson does three things, and they map one-to-one onto the framework in your reels:

  • Speed. They show up instantly. Nobody waits at a closed door.
  • Clarity. They say what they offer and what to do next, in plain words.
  • Proof. They show evidence that they have done it before, so a stranger can relax.

Say it your way

"Your website is your hardest working employee. The question is whether it is actually doing the job, or just standing there looking nice."

One more piece of the foundation: people do not read websites, they scan them. They arrive with a question, glance around for two or three seconds, and either see the answer or leave. That is why every fix in this pack is about removing the need to think, not adding more information.

02 · Reel 1

Speed: the three second window

When someone taps a link, there is a moment where the screen is still blank. That gap has a name: load time. The moment the first real content appears is called the first paint. You do not need to explain the technical causes on camera. You need to own one fact:

53%

of mobile visitors leave if a site takes more than 3 seconds to load.

Google mobile speed study

Sit with that number for a second, because it is wild. Half your potential customers can be gone before your website even appears. They never saw your logo, your photos, or your offer. They judged nothing. They just left. That is the invisible part of the problem: slow sites do not get complaints, they get silence.

Why slow feels like broken

On a phone, on holiday wifi, on mobile data in the car, three seconds of blank screen feels identical to "this site is down." People do not think "I will patiently wait for this small business." They think "next," and tap back to Google or Instagram where your competitor is one thumb-swipe away. Speed is not a technical nicety. Speed is the front door being open or closed.

What actually makes sites slow

You only need the plain-language versions: photos uploaded straight from a camera at full size, themes stuffed with features nobody uses, and cheap hosting far away from the visitor. The fix is equally plain: smaller images, less junk, better hosting. You never need to say "compression" or "CDN" on camera. "We make it load before your customer can blink" does the job.

Say it your way

"Nobody complains about a slow website. They just leave. That is what makes it dangerous, you never hear about the customers you lost."

03 · Reel 2

Clarity: the five second test

The five second test is the simplest website diagnosis that exists, and it is yours now. Show anyone a homepage for five seconds, take it away, and ask three questions:

  • What do they sell?
  • Who is it for?
  • What am I supposed to do next?

Miss even one answer and the site fails, because a real visitor gives you about that long before deciding to stay or leave. This works on any website, live, with no tools. You can do it on someone's phone at a networking event. It is your party trick and your sales opener in one.

Why visitors leave when they have to guess

Every time a visitor has to figure something out, they pay a small mental tax. The industry word for that tax is friction. A vague headline is friction. A menu with twelve options is friction, because more choices means slower decisions, and online, slow decisions become no decision at all. People do not push through friction on a stranger's website. They leave, not because the business is bad, but because guessing is effort and the back button is free.

The three-part fix

  • One headline that says what you sell. Not a slogan, not a vibe. "Custom windsurfing sails, built on Bonaire" beats "Ride the wind" every single time. Clear beats clever.
  • One action that is impossible to miss. Every page should have one obvious button: book, order, message us. This is the call to action, the moment the salesperson asks for the sale. A page without one is a pitch that ends with "anyway, see you around."
  • Zero guessing. If a stranger knows what, for whom, and what to do next within five seconds, you pass.

Say it your way

"A confused visitor never buys. They do not email you to ask what you meant. They just close the tab."

04 · Reel 3

Proof: why strangers believe you

A visitor who lands on your site has one silent question running the whole time: can I trust these people? They cannot see your face or your workshop. All they can judge is the screen.

75%

of people judge a company's credibility by its website alone.

Stanford web credibility research

Three quarters of your first impressions are decided by pixels. A dated, slow, or vague site does not read as "small business with character." It reads as "maybe they are not really in business anymore." Fair or not, the site is the storefront window, and people judge the shop by the window.

Proof is evidence, shown up front

The industry calls it social proof: real reviews, real client names, real photos of real work, recognizable logos, numbers that mean something. "34-car booking platform on Aruba" is proof. "We deliver quality solutions" is noise, because anyone can type it. The test for proof is simple: could a competitor copy-paste this sentence onto their site? If yes, it proves nothing.

The three failures in the breakdown reel

  • A hero that says nothing. The hero is the big top section, the first thing anyone sees. Filling it with a vague slogan wastes the most valuable screen space the business owns.
  • Twelve menu choices. Choice overload. The visitor came to do one thing and got handed a puzzle instead.
  • Zero proof. No reviews, no work, no faces, no numbers. The trust question stays unanswered, so the answer defaults to no.

Say it your way

"You never get to explain yourself to the visitors who left. Your website does all the explaining, so give it evidence, not slogans."

My customers find me on Instagram anyway.

You know your business, so your brain fills in every gap automatically. A stranger has no gaps filled. That is why the five second test uses someone else's eyes, never your own. Offer to run it together, it takes one minute.

11 · The door openers

The questions that open doors

Each question comes with why it works and what to listen for. The goal is never to interrogate. It is to ask the one thing that makes the owner think "nobody ever asked me that, and it is exactly my problem."

"When did you last Google your own business, like a customer would?"

Almost nobody has recently, and everyone reaches for their phone the moment you ask. Whatever appears, you now have a shared screen and a live diagnosis instead of a pitch. Listen for surprise: surprise is the sale starting.

"What is the one thing you want a visitor to do on your site?"

Most owners have never chosen. If they list four things, you have found the clarity problem, and you can say gently: "when everything is important, nothing is." Listen for hesitation, it tells you the site was built without a job description.

"Your last five new customers, where did they actually come from?"

Not "where does marketing come from" in general, the last five, by name. Owners always know. The answer tells you whether the website matters yet in their world, and keeps you honest: if all five came from word of mouth, say so, and build on that instead.

"Can I open your site on my phone right now, next to you?"

The live five second test, done together. You are not criticizing, you are both just watching what happens. Count the seconds out loud, kindly. Whatever loads slowly or reads vaguely does your arguing for you.

"What do people call or message you about that your website should be answering?"

The insider question. Every owner has a list they are tired of repeating: prices, hours, availability, parking. You just showed them their website's real job description, written by their own customers. Listen for the sigh, the sigh is the brief.

01 · The foundation

Nobody buys software. They buy their evenings back.

Pack 01 had one mental model: a website is a salesperson. This pack has one too, and it is even more important because it is what separates you from every agency selling "digital solutions":

Businesses do not have software problems. They have bottleneck problems. Software is just the wrench.

A bottleneck is the one narrow point everything has to squeeze through. In a small business, that point is almost always a person, and usually it is the owner. Every booking confirmed by hand. Every invoice typed twice. Every question answered personally at 10 at night. The business cannot grow past the speed of that one person's thumbs.

This is why you never open a conversation with technology. You open with the squeeze: "What is the task you repeat every single day that a machine should be doing?" The owner always knows the answer instantly. They have just never heard anyone call it fixable.

Say it your way

"I do not sell software. I find the one point where your whole business gets stuck behind your phone, and I remove it."

One more foundation idea: the duct-tape stage. Almost every growing small business runs on the same stack: WhatsApp, a spreadsheet, a paper notebook, and the owner's memory. It works at five customers a week. It cracks at twenty-five. Nobody chose that system, it just accumulated. Your job is to be the person who says: this is normal, everyone gets here, and there is a next stage.

02 · Diagnosis

Bottlenecks: how to spot the real problem

You already have the five second test from Pack 01. Here is the equivalent for operations, and it is just as sticky. Call it the phone test:

If the business stops when the owner's phone is off, the owner is the bottleneck.

Ask an owner: could you take a two week holiday, phone off, and come back to a business that ran normally? Watch their face. That face is your sales conversation. It works because it measures the real thing: how much of the operation lives in one person's head and hands.

The four signs, in plain language

  • Double entry. The same information typed into two or more places. A booking noted in WhatsApp, then in the spreadsheet, then on the invoice. Every copy is a chance for a mistake, and mistakes with money or dates cost real customers.
  • The human router. One person whose actual job has become forwarding messages between customers, staff and systems. That is a job software does for free, instantly, without ever being asleep.
  • No single source of truth. Ask "is the red scooter available Saturday?" and the answer requires checking three places and one memory. When nobody is sure which list is the real list, the business runs on hope.
  • The 10 pm workload. Admin that happens after closing, because the day was spent doing the actual work. That is not dedication, that is a queue. Queues are bottleneck symptoms.

Say it your way

"Ask yourself one question: what stops working when your phone dies? That list is exactly what we fix."

03 · The fix, part one

Custom software: the tailor-made suit that got affordable

Off-the-shelf software is the ready-made suit: cheap to start, fits nobody perfectly, and you pay rent on it forever. Custom software is the tailor-made one: built around exactly how this business works, owned, not rented.

For decades the honest advice for small businesses was: take the ready-made suit, custom is for corporations. That advice just expired, and this is the genuinely new thing you get to say on camera: building custom software has become dramatically cheaper and faster. A booking platform that would have been a six figure corporate project a few years ago is now within reach of a rental shop on an island. You know this is true because you have done it, twice.

Why off-the-shelf quietly fails small businesses

  • The 80 percent trap. Ready-made tools do 80 percent of what the business needs, and the missing 20 percent is exactly their weird part, which is usually the part that makes them money. So the owner bends the business around the tool, or duct-tapes the gap with, you guessed it, WhatsApp and a spreadsheet.
  • Subscription pile-up. One tool for bookings, one for invoices, one for email, one for reviews. Each has a monthly fee, each has a login, and none of them talk to each other. So the human router job comes back, just wearing nicer clothes.
  • Per-seat pricing. Grows with the team, so success gets more expensive every month, forever.

Custom flips it: the software fits the business's weird part precisely, everything lives in one system, and there is no rent that climbs with headcount. The honest caveat, and saying it builds trust: custom is not right for everyone. A business whose process is completely standard should use standard tools. Custom is for businesses whose way of working is the advantage.

Say it your way

"Ready-made software makes your business fit the tool. We build the tool to fit your business. That used to be a luxury. It is not anymore."

04 · The fix, part two

AI and automation: rules versus judgment

Owners lump "AI" and "automation" into one scary cloud. You win the room by separating them cleanly, because the split is simple:

  • Automation is rules. When this happens, do that. When a booking is confirmed, send the confirmation email. When a rental ends tomorrow, send the return reminder. No intelligence needed, just reliability. Automation is decades old, boring, and worth an enormous amount of money.
  • AI is judgment. Tasks that need a bit of reading, writing or deciding: drafting a reply in the company's tone, sorting incoming requests by what they are actually about, pulling the name, dates and amounts out of a messy message, answering the same twenty questions customers always ask.

The order matters and almost everybody gets it wrong: automation first, AI second. Most businesses asking about AI actually need plain automation, and it is cheaper and more reliable. Telling a client "you do not need AI for this yet" is the most trust-building sentence in your arsenal, precisely because everyone else is selling the hype.

The safety rail: human in the loop

The fear under every AI question is loss of control: "will a robot talk nonsense to my customers?" The answer is a design principle called human in the loop: the system prepares the work, a person approves it with one tap. You have a perfect live example: at Little John Scooters, every booking request lands on the owner's phone and he taps approve. The machine does the running around, the human keeps the decision. Say it exactly that concretely and the fear evaporates.

Where a lost owner starts

For the business that "does not know where to start," the starting point is never a technology. It is a list. Sit down for an hour and write out the tasks the team repeats every day or every week. That hour of process mapping is the audit. The first project is then obvious: pick the repeated task that hurts most and is easiest to hand over. Deliver one quick win, earn the trust, then go after the bigger bottleneck. Never boil the ocean on project one.

Say it your way

"Automation is rules: when this happens, do that. AI is judgment: reading, writing, sorting. Most businesses need the rules first, and half of the AI stories you hear are actually just rules."

07 · Ammunition

The math to own cold

Pack 01 gave you research numbers to quote. This pack is different, and stronger: you do the arithmetic live, with the client's own numbers. Nobody argues with their own numbers. The formula is one line:

h × € × 52

Hours per week on a repeated task, times what an hour is worth, times 52 weeks. That is the yearly cost of the bottleneck.

The bottleneck bill
10 × €50

Worked example: 10 hours a week of admin at 50 an hour is 26,000 a year, every year, forever.

Two follow-up moves that make it land:

  • The mistake multiplier. Hand-typed data goes wrong sometimes, and one double-booked car or wrong invoice can cost more than a month of admin time, plus the review that follows. Errors are the expensive part of manual work, not the hours.

01 · The foundation

The translation rule

Every website term, no matter how technical, translates into exactly one of three things a client cares about:

  • More customers. Anything about being found, being trusted, or being chosen.
  • Looking professional. Anything about design, credibility, or keeping up with competitors.
  • Less hassle. Anything about updates, maintenance, reliability, or not having to call a developer.

Before you ever explain a term, decide which of the three it maps to, then lead with that. "SEO" is not "search engine optimization," it is "being the business people find when they search for what you sell." That is a more-customers sentence. The jargon comes second, if at all.

Say it your way

This rule is also your armor. When a client throws a term at you from a cold-call SEO company or a nephew who read a blog, you do not need the deepest technical answer. You need to calmly place the term in one of the three buckets and explain what it is worth there. That is what an expert sounds like.

02 · Experience

UX: the invisible service

UX stands for user experience, and it means one thing: how easy it is for a visitor to do what they came to do. Find the price. Book the thing. Call the number. Good UX is invisible, you only notice UX when it is bad, the same way you only notice a door handle when it does not work.

UI, user interface, is the visible layer: the buttons, colors and screens themselves. The distinction in one line: UI is what it looks like, UX is how it works when a real person in a hurry uses it. A site can be beautiful and still fail UX, that was the whole point of reel 3.

Responsive design, in one breath

Responsive means the site rearranges itself to work on every screen size. The reason it matters is not technical, it is behavioral: most visitors arrive on a phone, often while standing in line or lying on a beach chair. A site that only looks good on a laptop is a site optimized for the minority. When you check a client's site, check it on your phone first, always. That is where their customers are.

Say it your way

"Open your website on your phone right now. That is how most of your customers see it. If you have to pinch and zoom, so do they, and they will not."

03 · Findability

SEO: being findable

SEO means making sure your business shows up when someone searches for what you sell. Think of Google as the modern phonebook, except the phonebook decides your page order based on how useful and trustworthy your site looks. SEO is the work of earning a better spot.

The three plain ingredients

  • Words. Your site has to actually say what you do, in the words customers search with. A dive shop whose homepage says "experience the blue" will lose to one that says "diving courses in Bonaire." Google reads words, not vibes.
  • Trust. Google prefers sites that are fast, work on phones, and have been around, plus sites that other sites link to. Notice that half of SEO is just Pack 01 done well.
  • Local. For almost every client you will meet, local SEO is the part that pays: the Google Business Profile with the map, reviews, photos and opening hours. For a restaurant or rental shop, that box matters more than the website's position, and most owners have never touched theirs.

The two honest warnings

First: SEO is a garden, not a switch. It takes months to grow and it compounds. Anyone promising "number one on Google in two weeks" is either lying or doing something Google punishes later. Second: nobody can guarantee rankings, not even Google's own staff. Saying both of these out loud is how you instantly separate yourself from the cold-callers who plague every business owner's phone.

Say it your way

"Anyone who guarantees you number one on Google is lying to you, and I will happily be the one who says it. What we can do is make sure that when someone nearby searches for what you sell, you are genuinely in the race."

04 · Under the hood

The anatomy: domain, hosting, CMS, backend

Four terms cover the entire machinery. Learn them as a house:

  • Domain is the address: yourbusiness.com. It is rented yearly from a registrar, and whoever controls it controls where visitors end up.
  • Hosting is the plot of land: the computer where the site physically lives. Cheap hosting far away means slow loading, and you know from Pack 01 what slow costs.
  • Frontend is everything the visitor sees and touches. Backend is the machinery behind it: where bookings are processed, forms are received, data is stored. Visitors never see the backend, they only feel whether it works.
  • CMS, content management system, is the editing room: the dashboard where the owner changes text, photos and prices without calling a developer. When a client asks "can I update it myself?", they are asking for a CMS, they just do not know the word.

Two small terms that complete the picture: HTTPS is the padlock in the browser bar, it means the connection is encrypted, and browsers shame sites that lack it with a "not secure" warning, which is a credibility killer from Pack 01's world. Analytics is the site's scoreboard: how many people visited, where they came from, what they did. Without it, every marketing decision is a guess.

Say it your way

"Think of it as a house. The domain is the address, hosting is the land, the design is the facade, and the CMS is your own key so you can rearrange the furniture without calling the builder."

01 · The foundation

The mental model: the slot in the head

Strip away every agency word and branding is two simple things:

Your brand is what people say about you when you are not in the room. Positioning is the slot you occupy in a customer's head.

The head part matters because human memory is lazy. Customers do not store paragraphs about businesses, they store one slot: "the fancy one," "the cheap one," "the one for tourists," "the one my sister uses." If you have not chosen your slot, the market assigns you one, and the default slot is "one of the others." That is the most expensive slot there is, because "one of the others" competes on price only.

So branding is not decoration. It is the answer to one question, repeated everywhere: why you, instead of the others? The logo, the words, the colors are just the delivery trucks for that answer.

Say it your way

"Your brand is not your logo. It is the sentence a customer uses when they recommend you to a friend. Do you know what that sentence is? Because if you do not choose it, the market chooses for you."

03 · The choice

Positioning: choosing who it is for

Positioning is deciding three things on purpose: who it is for, what makes it different, and what you are willing to not be. The last part is the hard one and the important one. A brand that is for everyone is for no one, because "for everyone" reads as "nothing special" in a lazy human memory.

Why narrowing feels wrong and works anyway

Every owner fears the same thing: "if I say I am for X, I lose everyone who is not X." The reality runs the other way. The specialist attracts more of everyone, because being clearly for someone signals competence to all. People hire the wedding photographer for family shoots too. The dive shop "for beginners who are a bit nervous" gets experienced divers as well, because caring that much about beginners signals they care, full stop. Narrow the message, not the door.

The differentiator test

A differentiator is what makes a business genuinely different, and it must pass the same copy-paste test proof had to pass in Pack 01: if a competitor could truthfully claim it too, it is not a differentiator. "Quality and service" fails for everyone. "The only rental in town where every booking is confirmed within a minute, day or night" passes, and notice that one is built on systems, which is why Modules 2 and 5 sell each other.

Say it your way

"Being for everyone is the most expensive marketing mistake there is, because it makes you invisible to everyone. Pick who you are for. The others will still come, they will just come on your terms."

04 · The words

Messaging and perceived value

Messaging is the small set of sentences a business repeats everywhere: what you do, for whom, why you. Not a slogan, a working vocabulary. The test for good messaging is brutal and simple: could a stranger repeat your pitch to another stranger after hearing it once? If your own customers cannot repeat it, it does not exist.

The most common messaging disease is talking about yourself. "We are passionate about quality" is self-talk. "Your scooter is confirmed before you finish your coffee" is customer-talk. The fix is mechanical: start sentences with the customer's situation, not with "we."

Perceived value: the price tag written in pixels

Perceived value is what a customer assumes something costs before hearing the price. It is set by signals: the photos, the typography, the way the offer is described, the confidence of the prices themselves. Here is the sentence that makes it click for owners: people do not price-shop the specialist, they price-shop the generic. When a business looks like every other business, price becomes the only thing left to compare. Raising perceived value is often cheaper than lowering prices, and it is the only one of the two that compounds.

Say it your way

"Customers decide what you probably cost before you ever tell them. If your website looks like a bargain, they will treat your price as one. Looking premium is not vanity, it is pricing power."

05 · The look

Visual identity and consistency

Visual identity is the recognition system: logo, colors, typography, photo style. The key word is system. A logo is one item; an identity is the set of rules that makes everything from the Instagram post to the invoice recognizably one company. Those rules usually live in a brand kit or brand guidelines: a short document that says exactly which colors, fonts and logo versions to use, so the business looks the same no matter who makes the next flyer.

Why consistency is the multiplier

Trust is built by repetition: seeing the same name, look and tone again and again until it feels familiar, and familiar feels safe. Every inconsistency resets the counter. A business that looks different on its sign, its site and its socials is paying for three first impressions and collecting none of the compound interest. This is why consistency beats brilliance: a decent identity applied everywhere outperforms a beautiful one applied randomly.

And a straight answer for the "brand versus logo" confusion, because a client will ask: the logo is the flag, the brand is the country. Redesigning the flag does not change the country, which is exactly why a rebrand that only touches the logo changes nothing that customers feel.

Say it your way

"Trust is repetition. Same look, same words, same feeling, everywhere your customer meets you. Every time you show up looking different, you start over as a stranger."

01 · The foundation

The pipeline: one model for everything

Every marketing term you will ever hear describes one thing: moving people along a pipeline. The pipeline has four stations:

  • Strangers who have never heard of the business.
  • Visitors who looked: at the site, the profile, the shop window.
  • Leads who raised a hand: messaged, left an email, asked a question.
  • Customers who paid, and ideally come back and bring friends.

The industry word for this is a funnel, because each station holds fewer people than the one before. Every tactic, no matter how fancy it sounds, is just a tool for one specific gap: ads move strangers to visitors, a good website moves visitors to leads (that was Pack 01, notice it), follow-up moves leads to customers, and service moves customers to repeat customers.

This gives you the diagnostic question that cuts through every "we need more marketing" conversation: where does the pipeline actually leak? A business with lots of visitors and no bookings does not need more ads, it needs Pack 01. A business nobody has heard of does not need a prettier website first, it needs reach. Owners buy tactics randomly; you sell the leak.

Say it your way

"More marketing is almost never the answer. First find where people fall out: do they not know you, not trust you, or not follow through? Those are three different fixes, and two of them are cheaper than ads."

02 · The two engines

Organic vs paid: the garden and the faucet

There are exactly two ways to get strangers into the pipeline, and the difference is how they behave over time:

  • Organic is the garden: content, SEO, reviews, word of mouth. Slow to start, costs time instead of money, and it compounds: a good post, a strong review page, a well-ranked site keep working while you sleep. Stop tending it and it fades slowly.
  • Paid is the faucet: ads on Meta, Google, wherever. Instant flow, precisely adjustable, and it stops the second you stop paying. No compounding, pure rental.

Neither is better; they answer different questions. Paid answers "I need customers this month." Organic answers "I want customers every month without buying each one." Healthy businesses usually want both: the faucet to fill the gap while the garden grows. Saying it this way defuses the eternal "should I do ads or content" question in one breath, and it makes you sound like nobody else in their DMs.

One more plain truth owners deserve to hear: content is marketing. The reels you make, the answers you post, the before-and-afters you show, that is organic pipeline work: teaching to earn trust before asking for anything. You are living proof of the method while you explain it, which is the most comfortable sales position there is.

Say it your way

"Ads are a faucet: turn them on, water flows, turn them off, it stops. Content is a garden: slow, then suddenly everywhere. Most businesses need the faucet while the garden grows. What almost nobody needs is only the faucet, forever."

03 · Raised hands

Leads, lead magnets and the list you own

A lead is a person who raised a hand and left a way to reach them. Not a follower, not a like: a name with a contact route attached. This distinction matters because owners confuse audience with leads. Ten thousand followers you cannot message individually is applause. Forty people who DMed the word AUDIT is business.

A lead magnet is something genuinely useful, given away to make raising the hand easy: a free check, a guide, a price calculator. Your own DM keywords are exactly this: AUDIT gives a free three-fix review, BOTTLENECK gives a free automatable-or-not verdict. The rule that keeps lead magnets honest: it must be worth something even if the person never buys. Generosity is the marketing.

The list you own

Here is a plain truth that lands hard: followers are rented, an email list is owned. Instagram can change its algorithm, lock an account, or fade like every platform eventually does, and the audience goes with it. An email or phone list is the one audience nobody can take away. It feels old-fashioned, and it quietly outperforms almost everything, because it arrives in a place people actually check, from a sender they chose. For any client, "start collecting emails" is among the highest-value boring advice you can give.

Say it your way

"Followers are rented, your list is owned. If Instagram disappeared tomorrow, who could you still reach? That answer is the real size of your audience."

04 · The faucet, up close

Ads and remarketing, demystified

An ad campaign has three parts, and knowing them lets you talk about ads without ever running one on camera:

  • Targeting: who sees it. Platforms know age, place and interests, so a Bonaire rental can show ads only to people currently on, or headed to, Bonaire.
  • Creative: what they see: the image or video and the words. This is the part that decides everything, and it is Pack 01 and Module 2 knowledge, compressed to three seconds.
  • Budget: what you pay. Platforms sell attention by auction, so cost depends on who else wants the same eyeballs.

Two honest warnings that build instant trust. First, ads amplify what exists: send paid traffic to a site that fails the five second test and you are paying to be ignored, which is why "fix the site first, then run ads" is real advice and not upselling. Second, ads are a learning system: the platform needs some spend and some time to figure out who responds, so judging ads after three days and twenty euros is like judging a gym after one visit.

Remarketing, the "ads that follow you" mystery solved

Remarketing means showing ads specifically to people who already visited you. That is the whole trick behind "I looked at shoes once and now they follow me everywhere." It works because those people already know you, so they are one small nudge from becoming a lead. It is the cheapest, most efficient ad money there is, and explaining the mystery on camera is a wonderful reel: everyone has experienced it, almost nobody knows its name.

Say it your way

"You know those ads that follow you after you looked at something once? That is called remarketing, it is cheap, and it works because you were already interested. Your business can do that too, and most of your competitors have no idea."

05 · The memory

CRM: the memory of the business

CRM stands for customer relationship management, but the useful translation is: the memory of the business. Who asked about a rental last month and never booked? Who came three times last year and deserves a call before high season? A CRM is where that lives, so following up stops depending on the owner remembering.

The connection to everything else in the Academy: leads leak between the raised hand and the sale, and the leak is almost always follow-up. The lead who messaged on Tuesday and got a reply on Friday bought elsewhere on Wednesday. A CRM plus simple automation (Pack 02: rules, not AI) fixes exactly this: every hand raised gets an answer, a reminder, a follow-up, without anyone's memory involved. And notice: a CRM is the phone test applied to marketing. If all the customer knowledge lives in the owner's head and WhatsApp scroll, the owner is the bottleneck again, just on the sales side.

Two more terms complete the picture. Acquisition is everything spent getting a new customer; retention is keeping the ones you have. The plain math every owner feels immediately: selling again to a happy customer costs almost nothing, while every new stranger must be bought or earned. Which is why the boring trio, list, CRM, follow-up, so often beats another round of ads.

Say it your way

"Most businesses do not have a getting-customers problem, they have a forgetting-customers problem. The people who almost booked, the ones who loved it last year, nobody follows up. That is not a marketing budget issue, that is a memory issue, and memory is exactly what software is good at."

The mental model: the fast junior

AI is a very fast, very well-read junior employee with infinite patience and no judgment of its own. You do not marvel at it. You manage it.

Every part of that sentence carries weight. Very fast: it drafts in seconds what takes a person an hour. Well-read: it has absorbed more text than any human ever will. 02 · The machine

What a model actually is

The engine behind modern AI is an LLM, a large language model. The plain version: software that has read an enormous amount of text and learned the patterns of language so well that it can continue any text sensibly. Ask a question, it writes the most fitting continuation: an answer. That is genuinely the core of it, and you can say so on camera without dumbing anything down.

Three consequences follow, and knowing them makes you sound like someone who actually works with this daily, which you are:

  • Models are brands. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini: different companies' models, like car brands. They differ in strengths and price, but they are the same kind of machine. A client asking "which AI should we use" is asking a car-brand question, and the honest answer is: for your task, several would work, the setup around it matters more than the badge.
  • A model knows the world, not your business. It has read the internet, not your price list, your policies, or your tone. Making AI useful for a specific business is mostly the work of feeding it that context. This is why "just use ChatGPT" disappoints and a configured setup does not, and it is precisely the gap where your service lives.
  • A model has a memory span, not a memory. It sees what is in the current conversation, the context, and nothing else, unless a system around it stores and retrieves things. When a client wants AI that "knows all our customers," they are asking for AI plus a database, which is Module 5 shaking hands with this one.

Say it your way

"Out of the box, AI knows the whole world and nothing about your business. The valuable part is not the AI, it is teaching it your prices, your tone and your rules. That is the difference between a clever toy and a colleague."

03 · The instruction

Prompting: briefing, not magic words

A prompt is simply the instruction you give an AI. The internet treats prompting as sorcery with secret spells. The truth is more useful: prompting is briefing, and the rules are the ones you already know from briefing people. A good brief says who the AI should act as, what the task is, who it is for, what good looks like, and what to avoid. Vague brief, generic work. Sharp brief, sharp work. Garbage in, garbage out, same as with any junior.

The one non-obvious rule worth teaching: show, do not describe. "Write it in our tone" does little, because the model has never heard your tone. Pasting two examples of how the business actually writes does everything. Examples beat adjectives, every time.

This section is also your education product in miniature. "Better briefing" is a teachable skill that owners can learn in an afternoon, it makes them measurably better with any AI tool they already pay for, and teaching it positions you as the person to call when they outgrow the afternoon. Generosity as marketing, Module 3 style.

Say it your way

"There are no magic words. Brief the AI like you would brief a new employee on their first day: who you are, what the task is, what good looks like, and two examples. That one habit beats every prompt trick list on the internet."

04 · The ladder

The ladder: chatbot, assistant, agent, workflow

AI systems come in levels of independence. Owners hear the words interchangeably; you will place them on a ladder, bottom to top:

  • Chatbot: answers questions in a chat window. Knows what it was given, does nothing on its own. The receptionist who can only talk.
  • Assistant: does tasks when asked: draft this email, summarize this document, sort these requests. Works on demand, one task at a time.
  • Agent: the word of the moment, so know it precisely: an AI that can take steps and use tools to reach a goal. Not just "write a reply" but look up the booking, check the calendar, draft the answer, and put it ready for approval. It acts, where the others only respond.
  • Workflow: a fixed chain of steps where AI handles some links and rules handle the rest. Request comes in, AI reads and sorts it, rules route it, AI drafts the reply, a human taps approve. Most real business value lives here, in boring reliable chains, not in a genius robot.

The higher on the ladder, the more independence, and therefore the more supervision the design needs. Which is why human in the loop, from Pack 02, is not a beginner feature that fades away: it is how professionals run agents. The machine climbs the ladder, the human keeps the yes. Little John remains your living example, one tap and all.

Say it your way

"A chatbot talks. An assistant does a task when you ask. An agent takes the steps itself: looks things up, prepares the work, puts it ready for your yes. The trick is not building the smartest one, it is building the right rung of the ladder for the job."

05 · The fine print

The honest limits: hallucination and trust

The single most important AI term for you to own is hallucination: when a model states something false with complete confidence. It happens because the machine continues text plausibly, and plausible is not the same as true. It does not know it is wrong; it does not know anything in the human sense. Every real AI system is designed around this fact, not in denial of it.

Being loudly honest about hallucination is your competitive weapon, exactly like the SEO honesty in Module 1. The hype sellers hide the fine print, you open with it: "AI states nonsense with confidence sometimes, here is how we build so that never reaches your customer." The design answers, so you can name them fluently: keep a human on the yes for anything customer-facing, ground the AI in the business's real data so it quotes your price list instead of inventing one, and give it permission to say "I do not know, a colleague will answer this." That last one sounds small and does the most.

Second honest topic, because every smart owner asks: where does my data go? The grown-up answer: reputable AI providers offer business terms where your data is not used to train their models, and the real risk in practice is usually sloppier than that, staff pasting customer details into random free tools. The fix is having a policy and picked tools instead of a quiet free-for-all. Offering to set that up is, again, a service.

Say it your way

"AI sometimes states nonsense with full confidence. Anyone selling you AI without mentioning that is selling you the brochure. The craft is building it so the nonsense never reaches a customer: real data underneath, and a human on the send button."

01 · The foundation

The three layers: every system, ever

Every piece of business software on earth, from a webshop to a hospital system, is the same three layers:

  • A place where data lives. The database: customers, bookings, prices, history.
  • Rules that move the data. The backend: what happens when a booking comes in, when a payment lands, when a date passes.
  • Screens people touch. The frontend: the website customers see, and the dashboard the team uses.

Once you see the three layers, you can decompose any tool a client mentions, any feature they wish for, any problem they describe. "Can customers see live availability?" is a question about layer one feeding layer three. "Can it email people automatically?" is a layer-two rule. "Two systems that do not talk" is two separate data layers that need a bridge. You are never lost in a technical conversation again, because everything lands in one of three boxes.

Say it your way

"Every system is three things: a place your information lives, rules that move it around, and screens people touch. When something feels broken in a business, one of those three is missing or doubled. Finding which one is my job."

02 · Layer one

The database: one filing cabinet, always right

A database is a structured filing cabinet: every customer, booking and payment in its own labeled drawer, instantly findable, never mislaid. The word structured is what separates it from the spreadsheet it usually replaces. A spreadsheet trusts humans to type carefully; a database enforces the rules: a booking must have a date, a customer must have contact details, the same scooter cannot be in two drawers at once.

This is where Pack 02's single source of truth stops being a slogan and becomes a physical thing: it is a database that all the screens read from. When the website, the owner's phone and the counter computer all show the same availability, that is not magic, that is three frontends reading one filing cabinet. And the reverse explains the chaos you meet in the wild: a business with a paper agenda, a WhatsApp thread and a spreadsheet has three filing cabinets that disagree, and every disagreement is a double booking waiting for a busy Saturday.

Say it your way

"A double booking is never bad luck. It is two lists disagreeing about one scooter. The fix is not being more careful, it is having one list that everything reads from."

03 · The bridges

APIs, webhooks and integrations

Three terms cover how systems talk to each other, and they are best learned as a tiny story:

  • An API is the official plug a system offers so other software can connect: ask it questions, give it instructions. "Does this payment provider have an API?" simply means "can other software talk to it, or is it a closed box?"
  • A webhook is the doorbell: instead of you asking constantly, the system rings you the moment something happens. Payment received, ring. Form submitted, ring. It is the difference between refreshing your mail and getting a push notification.
  • An integration is the finished bridge built from those parts: payment lands, webhook rings, backend rule marks the booking paid, confirmation email goes out. No human router anywhere in the chain, which is exactly the Pack 02 job description.

The practical judgment this gives you: when a client wants two tools connected, the first question is always "do both sides have a plug?" Good modern tools do, and then the bridge is honest work. A closed box without an API is the real warning sign, and telling a client "this tool of yours is a closed box, factor that into whether you keep it" is advisor-grade advice most owners have never received.

Say it your way

"When systems talk to each other, a human stops being the messenger. The payment tells the booking, the booking tells the email, and nobody types anything twice. That is all an integration is: your tools finally on speaking terms."

04 · The showcase

Anatomy of a booking system

The booking system is your flagship product, and it happens to be the perfect teaching object because it uses every layer and every bridge. Learn its anatomy as five organs, and you can whiteboard it for any client in two minutes:

  • Availability. The database knows every vehicle and every date. This is the single source of truth; everything else consults it.
  • The request. The customer-facing flow where a visitor picks dates and asks. Structured, so requests arrive as clean data instead of loose messages that need decoding.
  • The approval. The human-in-the-loop moment: the request lands with the owner, one tap says yes. Judgment stays human, legwork does not.
  • The notifications. Backend rules doing the running: confirmation to the customer, reminder before pickup, follow-up after return. Every email a human used to type at 10 pm.
  • The back office. The team's dashboard: fleet, calendar, customers, money, one screen. The control room behind the website.

Two more terms live naturally here. An internal tool is software only the team ever sees, like that back office; half of all bottleneck-removal is internal tools, invisible to customers and transformative anyway. A dashboard is any screen that shows the state of things at a glance; the test of a good one is that the owner checks it instead of calling someone.

Say it your way

"A booking system is five organs: one list that knows what is free, a clean way to ask, one tap to approve, emails that send themselves, and a control room for the team. Miss an organ and a human is quietly doing that organ's job by hand."

05 · The grown-up topics

The trust topics: backups, security, exit

Smart clients ask three sober questions before buying custom software. Having crisp answers is what separates a professional from an enthusiast, and all three answers happen to be your genuine policy:

  • What if it breaks? Serious systems are monitored, and uptime, the share of time everything runs, is a thing you can actually see rather than hope about. Things do occasionally break everywhere, Google included; the professional difference is knowing within minutes and having a fix path, instead of finding out from an angry customer.
  • What about our data? Two words carry this: backups, automatic copies so a bad day cannot erase the business's memory, and access, meaning logins per person with sensible permissions, so the Saturday helper cannot delete the fleet. Most small businesses have neither, which makes this both a risk conversation and a service.
  • What if we part ways? The exit answer from Module 1, now for software: the client owns their accounts, their data can be exported, and the system is documented so another developer could take over. Saying this unprompted wins deals, because every owner has heard a hostage story.

One last term because it comes up in every takeover conversation: a migration is moving data from an old system to a new one, carefully, without losing history. It is routine work with a scary name, and saying exactly that sentence to a nervous client is worth an hour of reassurance.

Say it your way

"Ask any software builder three things: what happens when it breaks, where do the backups live, and what happens if we part ways. If any answer is vague, walk. Mine are: I know before you do, automatic and daily, and everything is yours in writing."

01 · The shape

The conversation spine

Every good sales conversation has the same spine, and it is mostly listening. Six moves, in order:

  • Listen. Let them describe the problem in their words, fully, without steering. The words they choose are diagnostic gold.
  • Dig. Ask about the day-to-day reality, not about technology. What happens when a customer wants to book? Who answers? How long does it take? What goes wrong?
  • Diagnose. Silently place what you heard on the diagnosis map below. The stated problem and the real problem are different roughly half the time.
  • Translate. Say the real problem back in their language, using their examples. This is the moment trust is built or lost: when an owner hears their own situation described more clearly than they described it, the sale is mostly done.
  • Recommend. One thing, not a menu. The smallest move that fixes the biggest leak, with the bottleneck math or pipeline logic behind it.
  • Close-lite. A concrete, small next step with a date. Never "think about it and let me know." Always "I will send you X by Thursday, and if it looks right, we start with Y."

The ratio that decides everything

In a good discovery conversation the prospect talks about two thirds of the time. If you catch yourself explaining before you have asked at least four questions, you are pitching, not diagnosing, and pitches get price-shopped. Diagnoses get hired.

02 · The toolkit

The golden questions

You never need a script, you need a pocketful of questions that open the right doors. These eight cover nearly every conversation, and you already know the modules behind them:

  • "Walk me through what happens when a customer wants to book / order / hire you." The single best opener. The answer contains the workflow, the bottleneck, and the mood, all at once.
  • "What is the task you repeat every single day that you wish would just happen?" Pack 02's bottleneck finder. Owners answer instantly.
  • "Could you turn your phone off for two weeks?" The phone test. Watch the face, not the answer.
  • "Where do your customers come from right now?" Opens the pipeline. Also reveals what they believe about their own marketing, which is often more useful.
  • "When people compare you to others, what do they compare on?" If the answer is price, Module 2 just entered the room.
  • "What have you already tried, and what happened?" Prevents recommending the thing that already failed, and surfaces the burns that shape their objections.
  • "If this problem disappeared tomorrow, what would you do with the time?" Turns pain into a picture. People buy the picture.
  • "Who owns your domain, your accounts, your data?" Module 1's trust bomb. Often the moment they realize you are on their side.

The follow-up habit that beats any clever question: ask "what does that look like on a normal Tuesday?" Owners describe their business in ideals and exceptions; the truth lives in the boring Tuesday. Whenever an answer feels vague, send it to Tuesday.

04 · Discipline

The four rules of the room

  • Talk less than the prospect. The two-thirds ratio. Questions are your talking.
  • Never name a technology before naming the problem. "You need a CRM" before the diagnosis is pitching. "Leads go quiet because nobody follows up within the hour, and there is a tool category for exactly that" is advising. Same content, opposite trust.
  • One recommendation, not a menu. A menu makes the prospect the expert, which they are not, which is why they freeze. You were hired to have an opinion. Smallest move, biggest leak, one clear reason.
  • Always end with a concrete next step and a date. Momentum is a deliverable. "I will send the audit Thursday" beats a brilliant conversation that ends in fog.

The posture

You are not trying to win the deal. You are trying to be the most useful fifteen minutes of that owner's month. Do that honestly and the deals chase you, which is also the only version of sales that feels good on camera and off.

05 · The game

How the Arena works

You start a session and say "run me a prospect", or name one from the roster below, like "run the dive shop". From that moment I am the owner, fully in character, and here is the important part: each prospect has a hidden file this page does not show you. A surface complaint they open with, a real problem underneath, a burn from the past shaping their objections, and a budget mood. You get what a real conversation gets you: whatever your questions earn.

Difficulty levels

  • Friendly: open, chatty, answers what you ask. For learning the spine.
  • Skeptical: burned before, short answers, at least one hard objection. The default.
  • Chaotic: rambles, self-diagnoses wrongly, asks the price in minute one, changes subject. Closest to real life.

Say the level when you start, like "run the realtor, chaotic." You can also ask for a curveball prospect not on the roster, or hand me a real business you are actually pitching this week, which turns the Arena into rehearsal, the best use of all.

The review

Say "end scene" any time, or finish the conversation naturally. I break character and we review the tape: what you diagnosed versus what the hidden file said, which golden questions you used and skipped, where you pitched instead of asked, how the objection handling landed, and whether the recommendation fit. Then the score, honest, using the rubric below.

08 · The secret weapon

Questions that contain proof

Here is the mechanism behind "she has done this before": a question can carry more proof than a portfolio. When you ask about a moment only insiders know exists, the prospect concludes, without you claiming anything, that you have stood where they stand. No slide can do that, because slides are you talking about yourself, and insider questions are you demonstrating knowledge of their life.

The formula: name a painful, specific moment from their operational reality, and ask how they handle it. Not "do you have challenges with bookings" (generic, salesy, forgettable) but "when someone returns the scooter late and the next renter is standing there, what happens?" The second version proves you know that moment exists. Every trade has these moments, and collecting them is now part of your craft: every client project, every Arena run, every reel comment teaches you new ones.

The three finishing touches

  • Ask, then be quiet. The power is in the silence after. The prospect fills it with their story, and their story is your brief. An insider question answered by you is just a pitch again.
  • Normalize honestly. After they confess the chaos: "you are not the exception, this is what we find in almost every business at your stage." Only say it because it is true. Shame closes people, normalcy opens them.
  • Reflect before you recommend. Repeat their two or three worst moments back in their own words before proposing anything. Feeling understood comes before feeling helped, always, and cannot be skipped.

In the Arena

From now on I score insider questions explicitly: using at least two per conversation, matched to the prospect's trade, earns full Discovery marks. Generic discovery ("tell me about your challenges") caps the score, exactly like it caps trust in real life.

09 · The receipts

Deploying a story in thirty seconds

Case studies do not belong in a monologue, they belong in the gaps a conversation opens. The shape is situation, turn, takeaway, thirty seconds, and it always ends by handing the conversation back:

The template, worked

Situation: "A rental owner we work with used to confirm every booking himself, from his phone, at all hours."

Turn: "We built it so requests come in structured, land on his phone, and one tap approves. Confirmations and reminders send themselves, in his branding."

Takeaway plus handback: "He kept every yes and lost the running around. Does any of that sound like your evenings?"

The rules that keep stories working for you instead of against you:

  • One story per conversation, matched to their wound. A second story starts to feel like a brochure. Pick the one that mirrors what they just told you.
  • Only numbers you can stand behind. "He got his evenings back" is honest and lands. An invented percentage is a landmine that goes off in the reference call. Real and modest beats inflated and shaky, in content and in sales chairs alike.
  • Name clients only with their blessing. Anonymous versions, "a rental on Aruba," lose nothing; the situation does the convincing, not the name.
  • End with a question, never with the moral. "Sound familiar?" turns your proof into their story, and the conversation keeps belonging to them, which is where sales are won.

In the Arena

Story deployment is now scored inside Recommendation: one fitting story, told in under forty seconds, ending with a handback question, earns the bonus. Two stories, any bragging tone, or a story that ignores what the prospect actually said, costs it.
