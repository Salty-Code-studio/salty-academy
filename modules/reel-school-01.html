01 · The foundation

The one mental model behind everything

A website is not a brochure. It is a salesperson that works every hour of every day.

Everything else in this pack hangs on that single idea. When you judge a website, do not ask "is it pretty?" Ask "would I hire this person to sell for me?" A salesperson who takes forever to answer the door, mumbles about themselves instead of listening, and never asks for the sale would be fired in a week. Most websites are exactly that salesperson.

A good salesperson does three things, and they map one-to-one onto the framework in your reels:

  • Speed. They show up instantly. Nobody waits at a closed door.
  • Clarity. They say what they offer and what to do next, in plain words.
  • Proof. They show evidence that they have done it before, so a stranger can relax.

Say it your way

"Your website is your hardest working employee. The question is whether it is actually doing the job, or just standing there looking nice."

One more piece of the foundation: people do not read websites, they scan them. They arrive with a question, glance around for two or three seconds, and either see the answer or leave. That is why every fix in this pack is about removing the need to think, not adding more information.

02 · Reel 1

Speed: the three second window

When someone taps a link, there is a moment where the screen is still blank. That gap has a name: load time. The moment the first real content appears is called the first paint. You do not need to explain the technical causes on camera. You need to own one fact:

53%

of mobile visitors leave if a site takes more than 3 seconds to load.

Google mobile speed study

Sit with that number for a second, because it is wild. Half your potential customers can be gone before your website even appears. They never saw your logo, your photos, or your offer. They judged nothing. They just left. That is the invisible part of the problem: slow sites do not get complaints, they get silence.

Why slow feels like broken

On a phone, on holiday wifi, on mobile data in the car, three seconds of blank screen feels identical to "this site is down." People do not think "I will patiently wait for this small business." They think "next," and tap back to Google or Instagram where your competitor is one thumb-swipe away. Speed is not a technical nicety. Speed is the front door being open or closed.

What actually makes sites slow

You only need the plain-language versions: photos uploaded straight from a camera at full size, themes stuffed with features nobody uses, and cheap hosting far away from the visitor. The fix is equally plain: smaller images, less junk, better hosting. You never need to say "compression" or "CDN" on camera. "We make it load before your customer can blink" does the job.

Say it your way

"Nobody complains about a slow website. They just leave. That is what makes it dangerous, you never hear about the customers you lost."

03 · Reel 2

Clarity: the five second test

The five second test is the simplest website diagnosis that exists, and it is yours now. Show anyone a homepage for five seconds, take it away, and ask three questions:

  • What do they sell?
  • Who is it for?
  • What am I supposed to do next?

Miss even one answer and the site fails, because a real visitor gives you about that long before deciding to stay or leave. This works on any website, live, with no tools. You can do it on someone's phone at a networking event. It is your party trick and your sales opener in one.

Why visitors leave when they have to guess

Every time a visitor has to figure something out, they pay a small mental tax. The industry word for that tax is friction. A vague headline is friction. A menu with twelve options is friction, because more choices means slower decisions, and online, slow decisions become no decision at all. People do not push through friction on a stranger's website. They leave, not because the business is bad, but because guessing is effort and the back button is free.

The three-part fix

  • One headline that says what you sell. Not a slogan, not a vibe. "Custom windsurfing sails, built on Bonaire" beats "Ride the wind" every single time. Clear beats clever.
  • One action that is impossible to miss. Every page should have one obvious button: book, order, message us. This is the call to action, the moment the salesperson asks for the sale. A page without one is a pitch that ends with "anyway, see you around."
  • Zero guessing. If a stranger knows what, for whom, and what to do next within five seconds, you pass.

Say it your way

"A confused visitor never buys. They do not email you to ask what you meant. They just close the tab."

04 · Reel 3

Proof: why strangers believe you

A visitor who lands on your site has one silent question running the whole time: can I trust these people? They cannot see your face or your workshop. All they can judge is the screen.

75%

of people judge a company's credibility by its website alone.

Stanford web credibility research

Three quarters of your first impressions are decided by pixels. A dated, slow, or vague site does not read as "small business with character." It reads as "maybe they are not really in business anymore." Fair or not, the site is the storefront window, and people judge the shop by the window.

Proof is evidence, shown up front

The industry calls it social proof: real reviews, real client names, real photos of real work, recognizable logos, numbers that mean something. "34-car booking platform on Aruba" is proof. "We deliver quality solutions" is noise, because anyone can type it. The test for proof is simple: could a competitor copy-paste this sentence onto their site? If yes, it proves nothing.

The three failures in the breakdown reel

  • A hero that says nothing. The hero is the big top section, the first thing anyone sees. Filling it with a vague slogan wastes the most valuable screen space the business owns.
  • Twelve menu choices. Choice overload. The visitor came to do one thing and got handed a puzzle instead.
  • Zero proof. No reviews, no work, no faces, no numbers. The trust question stays unanswered, so the answer defaults to no.

Say it your way

"You never get to explain yourself to the visitors who left. Your website does all the explaining, so give it evidence, not slogans."

08 · Make it yours

Teach-back drills

Reading this makes it familiar. Explaining it makes it yours. Answer these out loud, in your own words, no peeking. Then, whenever you want, tell me your answers in a session and I will poke holes until every answer is bulletproof.

  1. Explain the five second test to a friend who owns a nail salon, in under thirty seconds.
  2. Why is a slow website more dangerous than an ugly one?
  3. What is the difference between a slogan and a value proposition? Give one example of each for a car rental.
  4. A client says their menu needs all twelve pages "because everything is important." What do you say?
  5. Name three things that count as proof and one thing that looks like proof but is not.
  6. Why does the audit offer work better than "contact us for a quote"?

Pushbacks you will actually get, and calm answers

My customers find me on Instagram anyway.
Instagram builds the interest, but the moment someone is ready to spend money, they check the website to see if you are legit. That is exactly the moment you cannot afford to look half-finished. The site does not replace Instagram, it catches what Instagram throws.
My site looks fine to me.
You know your business, so your brain fills in every gap automatically. A stranger has no gaps filled. That is why the five second test uses someone else's eyes, never your own. Offer to run it together, it takes one minute.
I do not have reviews yet.
Proof is broader than reviews. Real photos of real work, the number of jobs done, names of businesses you have helped, even one honest before-and-after. Start with what is true today, and never fake it, because fake proof reads as fake instantly.
Is three seconds not a bit exaggerated?
It feels exaggerated because you wait patiently for your own site. Your customer is on a phone, between two other apps, with your competitor one tap away. Google measured it across millions of sessions: past three seconds, half of them are gone.

One camera note

Before each take, say the beat's idea in one sentence to yourself, then record while explaining it as if a friend asked you about it two minutes ago. If you stumble, keep rolling and rephrase. The rephrase is usually the take you keep, because it is the one where you were thinking instead of reciting.

09 · Going deeper

Say it three ways

You are never locked into one register. For every core idea in this pack, have three versions ready: the pro version for a client who speaks the jargon, the everyday version for camera, and the kitchen-table version for someone who has never thought about websites for one minute in their life. Being able to slide between them is what fluency actually is.

The website's job

To a pro: "The site is your top-of-funnel conversion surface. If it does not convert, everything you spend on traffic is wasted downstream."

Everyday: "Your website is your hardest working salesperson. It just needs to actually do the job."

Kitchen table: "Imagine your shop with a window so dusty nobody can see inside, and a door that sticks. People walk past. That is what a bad website is. We clean the window and oil the door."

Conversion

To a pro: "Your conversion rate is the share of visitors who take the action. Doubling it beats doubling your ad spend, at a fraction of the cost."

Everyday: "Turning lookers into bookers."

Kitchen table: "Say ten people walk into a shop and one buys. If we change the shop so two of them buy, the owner just doubled the business without a single extra person walking in. That is all this is."

Proof

To a pro: "Social proof is your highest-leverage trust signal. Reviews, named clients and real numbers, placed where the visitor decides."

Everyday: "Evidence you have done this before, shown up front."

Kitchen table: "When two food stands sit next to each other and one has a line, you join the line. Nobody told you to. Your website needs a visible line in front of it: happy customers, real photos, real names."

The rule for choosing a register: listen to the words the other person uses first, then speak one notch simpler than they do. Simpler never offends. Jargon at the wrong moment always does.

10 · Evidence

Case files and the pain map

Case: the rental hero that said too much

Our own work on a car rental site on Aruba. The homepage tried to say everything at once, and so said nothing. We rebuilt the top of the page around real photography of the actual fleet, one headline about what you can rent, and one button: see our cars. No slogan, no mission statement, no carousel of ten messages. The lesson you can tell on camera: the rewrite was mostly deleting. Clarity is usually subtraction, and subtraction takes more courage than adding.

Picture this: the salon that lives on Facebook

A composite, not a client, and you will meet her within a month of posting content: a nail salon owner whose whole online presence is a Facebook page last updated eight months ago. New customers message at all hours asking for prices and openings, and she answers every one by hand, or misses them. She does not need a big website. She needs one fast page with prices, photos, opening hours and one booking button, so the twenty questions answer themselves. Small fix, huge relief. Keep this story in your pocket, because "one page that answers the questions you are tired of answering" is an offer almost nobody refuses.

11 · The door openers

The questions that open doors

Each question comes with why it works and what to listen for. The goal is never to interrogate. It is to ask the one thing that makes the owner think "nobody ever asked me that, and it is exactly my problem."

"When did you last Google your own business, like a customer would?"

Almost nobody has recently, and everyone reaches for their phone the moment you ask. Whatever appears, you now have a shared screen and a live diagnosis instead of a pitch. Listen for surprise: surprise is the sale starting.

"What is the one thing you want a visitor to do on your site?"

Most owners have never chosen. If they list four things, you have found the clarity problem, and you can say gently: "when everything is important, nothing is." Listen for hesitation, it tells you the site was built without a job description.

"Your last five new customers, where did they actually come from?"

Not "where does marketing come from" in general, the last five, by name. Owners always know. The answer tells you whether the website matters yet in their world, and keeps you honest: if all five came from word of mouth, say so, and build on that instead.

"Can I open your site on my phone right now, next to you?"

The live five second test, done together. You are not criticizing, you are both just watching what happens. Count the seconds out loud, kindly. Whatever loads slowly or reads vaguely does your arguing for you.

"What do people call or message you about that your website should be answering?"

The insider question. Every owner has a list they are tired of repeating: prices, hours, availability, parking. You just showed them their website's real job description, written by their own customers. Listen for the sigh, the sigh is the brief.
